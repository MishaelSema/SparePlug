## 1.2 Background of the Study

The automotive sector in Cameroon remains a vital pillar of the national economy, supporting transportation, logistics, and the daily movement of millions of people. Yet, behind the scenes of this bustling industry lies a persistent challenge that many enterprises have struggled to address effectively. Enterprises within this sector, such as John Automobile located in Mvog-Ada, Yaoundé, continue to manage their complex inventories and customer interactions using traditional, paper-based methods that were never designed for the scale of modern commerce. This section explores the historical evolution, underlying concepts, relevant theories, and the specific context that frames this research.

### 1.2.1 Historical Background

Over the decades, the automotive spare parts industry in Cameroon has grown from small, informal trading posts into a significant commercial sector. In the early days of major automotive hubs in Yaoundé, record-keeping was simple; transactions were logged in physical notebooks, and shop owners knew most of their customers by name. However, as the volume of imported parts increased and car models became more diverse and complex, these manual systems began to show their age.

Today, the market handles thousands of different parts, each with varying specifications, prices, and compatibility requirements. Despite this growth, most spare parts traders in Mvog-Ada still rely on handwritten ledgers and memory to manage their stock. The transition to digital systems has been painfully slow, hindered by factors such as the cost of commercial software licenses, limited technical skills among shop owners, and a general resistance to change in deeply rooted trading cultures. According to Owusu et al. (2022), technology adoption in developing economies often faces cultural and financial barriers that slow down digital transformation in traditional markets.

### 1.2.2 Conceptual Background

At its core, this project is about bringing modern web technology to a traditional market. A **web-based car parts availability and price comparison system** is a software application that allows users to search for automotive spare parts online, check whether those parts are in stock, and compare prices across multiple vendors, all from a single platform accessible through any web browser. Unlike simple classified ads or social media posts, a dedicated system like this integrates real-time inventory data, vehicle compatibility filtering, and structured pricing information into one coherent interface.

In this project, we refer to the system as **SparePlug** - a digital marketplace that connects car owners, mechanics, and spare parts vendors. The system is built to bridge the gap between the physical inventory sitting on shop shelves in Mvog-Ada and the digital world that customers increasingly inhabit. It enables vendors to list their products with accurate details and prices, while buyers can search, compare, and contact sellers directly without physically visiting multiple shops.

For a system like SparePlug to succeed among vendors and customers in Yaoundé, it must be perceived as genuinely useful and surprisingly easy to use, especially for people who may not be tech-savvy.

This puzzle motivates the entire research project and points toward a clear, practical solution: a web-based system that brings real-time visibility to inventory, transparent pricing, and efficient customer communication under one digital roof.

## 2.2 Theoretical Review

Theoretical frameworks serve as the intellectual scaffolding of any research project. They provide established models for understanding phenomena, evaluating outcomes, and interpreting results. This study draws from three major theoretical perspectives: the Technology Acceptance Model, the Information Systems Success Model, and the System Development Life Cycle methodology.

### 2.2.1 Technology Acceptance Model (TAM)

The **Technology Acceptance Model (TAM)** was developed by Fred Davis in 1989 as a theoretical framework for understanding why individuals accept or reject new technology. At its core, TAM proposes that a person's decision to use a new technology is primarily driven by two factors: **Perceived Usefulness (PU)** - the degree to which a person believes that using the technology will enhance their performance - and **Perceived Ease of Use (PEOU)** - the degree to which a person believes that using the technology will be free of effort.

In the context of this study, TAM is highly relevant. Vendors and customers at John Automobile may initially resist a digital platform if they perceive it as complicated or time-consuming to learn. However, if SparePlug demonstrates clear benefits - such as faster customer response times, more sales, and easier part searches - users are more likely to adopt it willingly. Davis (1989) argued that PU and PEOU directly influence a user's attitude toward using a system, which in turn influences behavioral intention and actual system use.

Subsequent research has expanded TAM significantly. Venkatesh and Davis (2000) extended the original model to include social influence and cognitive instrumental processes, producing TAM2. Later, Venkatesh et al. (2003) unified multiple models into the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT), which has become one of the most widely cited frameworks in information systems research.

Research specifically on TAM in African and developing-economy contexts has revealed important nuances. A study by Hossain, Hussain, and Akther (2023) published in the _Open Journal of Business and Management_ investigated e-commerce adoption in developing economies using an extended TAM framework. They found that trust, enjoyment, habit compatibility, social influence, and perceived risk all play significant roles in shaping behavioral intentions toward e-commerce platforms in developing countries. This finding is particularly relevant for SparePlug, where both vendors and buyers in Yaoundé may have concerns about digital trust, internet reliability, and cultural fit with existing market practices.

A study conducted in Lesotho by PALLI Publishers (2023) on e-commerce adoption using an adapted TAM identified attitude, convenience, time and cost savings, and perceived ease of use as enabling factors, while dependability, accessibility, and policy initiatives acted as inhibiting factors. Research by Naatu and Selormey (2024) published in the _International Journal of Emerging Markets_ confirmed that perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness are key predictors of technology adoption intent in Ghana, a neighboring West African country with a similarly developing digital economy. Their findings support the applicability of TAM to Sub-Saharan African contexts and reinforce the importance of designing user-friendly interfaces for technology solutions targeting this region.

### 2.2.2 Information Systems Success Model (DeLone and McLean)

The **Information Systems Success Model**, originally developed by William DeLone and Ephraim McLean in 1992 and updated in 2003, provides a comprehensive framework for evaluating the success of an information system. The updated model identifies six interrelated dimensions of IS success:

- **System Quality** - the technical quality of the system itself, including its performance, reliability, and ease of use.
- **Information Quality** - the quality of the information produced by the system, including accuracy, completeness, relevance, and timeliness.
- **Service Quality** - the quality of support provided to users by the system operator or service provider.
- **Intention to Use / Use** - the extent to which users actually use or intend to use the system.
- **User Satisfaction** - the overall satisfaction of users with the system.
- **Net Benefits** - the positive or negative impacts the system has on individuals, organizations, or society.

Applied to this study, the DeLone and McLean model guides the evaluation of SparePlug across multiple dimensions. Is the system fast and responsive? Are product listings accurate and up to date? Does the platform provide good support to vendors and buyers? Do users find value in the platform and return to use it again? These questions are central to assessing whether SparePlug achieves its intended purpose.

DeLone and McLean (2004) themselves applied their model specifically to e-commerce contexts in a study published in the _International Journal of Electronic Commerce_. They argued that the model provides a parsimonious framework for organizing e-commerce success metrics and demonstrated its applicability through two case examples. Their work confirms that for an e-commerce platform like SparePlug, information quality, system quality, and service quality are all critical success factors.

A study by Angelina, Hermawan, and Suroso (2019) published in the _Journal of Information Systems Engineering and Business Intelligence_ applied the DeLone and McLean model to three Indonesian e-commerce platforms. The findings confirmed that system quality and service quality significantly affect user satisfaction, and user satisfaction in turn drives net benefits. This empirical validation across e-commerce platforms strengthens the relevance of the model for SparePlug.

### 2.2.3 System Development Life Cycle (SDLC)

The **System Development Life Cycle (SDLC)** is a structured project management framework that describes the stages involved in planning, designing, building, testing, deploying, and maintaining a software system. The SDLC provides a systematic approach to software development that ensures quality, accountability, and predictability throughout the project lifecycle.

According to IBM (2024) and GeeksforGeeks (2025), the SDLC typically consists of seven distinct phases:

- **Planning** - Defining the project's goals, scope, resources, and feasibility.
- **Analysis** - Gathering and documenting detailed requirements from stakeholders.
- **Design** - Creating the system's architecture, database schema, and technical specifications.
- **Development** - Writing the actual code for the system.
- **Testing** - Systematically verifying that the system works as specified.
- **Implementation / Deployment** - Installing the system in a production environment.
- **Maintenance** - Ongoing monitoring, bug fixing, and enhancements after deployment.

For web-based systems specifically, the SDLC has been adapted to account for unique concerns such as cross-browser compatibility, mobile responsiveness, and security across internet protocols. Modern web development SDLCs frequently adopt agile or iterative models, which allow for incremental delivery and continuous feedback, resulting in faster time-to-market and greater flexibility.

In this study, the SDLC was applied as follows: the planning phase involved field visits and interviews at John Automobile; the analysis phase included the design and distribution of questionnaires and direct observation; the design phase produced system architecture diagrams and database schemas; the development phase involved building the system using PHP, MySQL, and Tailwind CSS; the testing phase included unit testing, integration testing, and user acceptance testing; and the implementation phase involved deploying the system for evaluation.

### 2.3.1 E-Commerce and Electronic Business

**E-commerce**, short for electronic commerce, refers to the buying and selling of goods and services over the internet. It encompasses a wide range of activities, from online retail stores and digital marketplaces to mobile commerce and social commerce. E-commerce enables businesses and consumers to conduct transactions across geographical boundaries using digital platforms, dramatically reducing the time and cost associated with traditional commerce.

The global e-commerce automotive aftermarket is a particularly dynamic sector. According to a 2026 market report published by _Global Growth Insights_, the global e-commerce automotive aftermarket was valued at approximately \$119.56 billion in 2025 and is projected to reach \$620.47 billion by 2035, reflecting a compound annual growth rate of 17.9%. Approximately 62% of vehicle owners now prefer online platforms for researching spare parts, while about 55% of independent repair workshops use digital marketplaces to source components.

In the context of developing economies, e-commerce adoption faces unique challenges and opportunities. A 2024 research paper published on arXiv titled _"E-Commerce in Africa: Divergent Impacts on Rural and Urban Economies"_ highlights that e-commerce in Africa is transforming rapidly, offering significant opportunities for economic growth and MSME development. The paper notes that barriers to e-commerce adoption in Africa include limited internet infrastructure, digital literacy gaps, payment system challenges, and cultural preferences for cash-on-delivery transactions.

### 2.3.2 Price Comparison Systems

A **price comparison system** is a specialized type of e-commerce platform that aggregates pricing information from multiple sellers or sources and presents it in a structured, comparable format to help buyers make informed purchasing decisions. Unlike traditional e-commerce stores where a single seller sets the price, price comparison platforms enable side-by-side evaluation across vendors, giving buyers power and transparency.

Platforms like **CarPartsPicker** (carpartspicker.com), a specialized price comparison engine for the automotive community, aggregate real-time data from trusted retailers including Amazon and eBay to bring users the lowest prices on every part. Similarly, **Big Broke Auto Parts Search** (bigbroke.com) helps users compare prices across multiple retailers by generating search links for specific vehicle and part requirements.

For the Cameroonian context, a price comparison system is particularly relevant. The informal negotiation culture in Yaoundé's spare parts markets means that customers often pay different prices for the same item depending on their bargaining skills. A digital platform that displays standardized prices from verified vendors eliminates this information asymmetry and levels the playing field for all buyers.

### 2.3.3 Multi-Vendor Marketplace Architecture

A **multi-vendor marketplace** is a digital platform that allows multiple independent sellers to list and sell products or services within a single storefront, with the platform operator managing the marketplace infrastructure, setting rules, and in some cases processing payments. Unlike a single-vendor e-commerce store, a marketplace must support complex interactions between multiple sellers and buyers simultaneously.

According to a guide published by **Hunchbite** (2026), building a multi-vendor e-commerce platform is fundamentally more complex than building a traditional single-vendor e-commerce website. Key architectural challenges include vendor onboarding and management, split payment processing, per-vendor inventory tracking, commission calculation logic, and a shopping experience that feels seamless even when products come from different sellers.

The technical architecture of a multi-vendor marketplace is typically organized into three layers: the **Frontend Layer** (the user-facing interface built with responsive design), the **Backend Services** (server-side logic managing vendor accounts, product listings, and inquiries), and the **Database Layer** (typically a relational DBMS like MySQL for ACID-compliant data management).

For SparePlug, the multi-vendor marketplace model is the natural choice because it enables the platform to serve not just John Automobile but multiple spare parts vendors across Yaoundé and eventually Cameroon.

### 2.3.4 Database Management Systems (DBMS) in Web Applications

A **Database Management System (DBMS)** is software that enables the creation, storage, maintenance, manipulation, and retrieval of data in a structured and organized manner. For web applications like SparePlug, relational database management systems (RDBMS) like **MySQL** are the most common choice. MySQL stores data in tables composed of rows and columns, with relationships established between tables through primary and foreign keys.

Proper database design follows normalization principles - typically up to the Third Normal Form (3NF) - to eliminate data redundancy and ensure data integrity. Indexes on frequently queried columns dramatically improve search performance. A robust database layer also ensures **ACID compliance** - Atomicity, Consistency, Isolation, and Durability - which is critical for e-commerce applications where financial transactions must be recorded accurately and reliably.

### 2.3.5 User Experience (UX) Design in Web Systems

**User Experience (UX) design** refers to the overall experience a user has when interacting with a digital product - encompassing usability, accessibility, efficiency, and satisfaction. For web-based systems targeting diverse user populations, UX design must account for varying levels of digital literacy, different device types, and connectivity constraints.

A key UX consideration for SparePlug is the **mobile-first approach**. Cameroon has very high mobile phone penetration - approximately 25.5 million active mobile connections as of early 2025, equivalent to 86.3% of the population (DataReportal, 2025). Most users in Yaoundé access the internet primarily through their mobile phones. Therefore, the SparePlug interface was designed to be fully responsive, rendering well on small mobile screens.

The shop serves a diverse customer base including individual car owners, professional mechanics, garage operators, and fleet managers.

### 2.5.3 Organizational Structure and Current Operations

John Automobile operates as a traditional family-managed business with a flat organizational structure. The current operational workflow follows a manual, paper-based process: a customer visits or calls, the clerk relies on memory or physical inspection to verify availability, pricing is determined ad hoc, transactions are recorded in a physical ledger, and new stock arrivals are recorded in a separate intake register.

### 2.5.4 Challenges and Motivation for Digital Transformation

Through direct observation and analysis of the manual workflow, the following specific operational challenges have been identified: price inconsistency (different clerks quoting different prices), inventory opacity (unknown stock levels), slow customer service (long physical searches), limited market reach (no digital presence), and data vulnerability (physical ledgers at risk of loss or damage). ## 3.2 Research Design

A **research design** is the overall plan or strategy that guides the researcher in collecting, analyzing, and interpreting data. According to Yin (2003), the choice of research design should be guided by three conditions: the type of research question posed, the extent of control the researcher has over behavioral events, and the degree of focus on contemporary as opposed to historical events.

Given that this study seeks to understand a contemporary phenomenon (manual spare parts management at John Automobile) within its real-world context, and involves no experimental control over behavioral events, this research adopts a **Case Study Research Design**.

A case study is defined by Runeson et al. (2008) as "a suitable research methodology for software engineering research since it studies contemporary phenomena in its natural context." The case study approach allows the researcher to investigate the problem in depth, using multiple sources of evidence, and to develop solutions grounded in the realities of the specific organizational setting.

The case study design was selected because the study focuses on a specific, real-world entity (John Automobile); the research questions are exploratory and explanatory; the research involves direct observation of existing processes; and the researcher had direct access to the case study site.

### 3.2.1 Research Philosophy

This study is grounded in a **positivist research philosophy**, which assumes that the social and technical world can be studied objectively and that findings can be measured and verified.

## 3.5 Analysis of the Existing (Manual) System

### 3.5.1 The Manual Workflow

- A customer arrives at the shop or calls by phone to inquire about a specific car part.
- The sales clerk mentally searches their memory or physically walks into the warehouse to check shelves.
- If found, the clerk quotes a price based on memory or ad hoc negotiation.
- If not found, the customer is turned away or asked to return later.
- If a sale is agreed, the transaction is recorded by hand in a physical sales ledger.
- New stock arrivals are recorded in a separate intake register.
- There is no systematic tracking of stock levels, no customer database, and no digital catalog.

### 3.5.2 Weaknesses of the Manual System

- **Information Asymmetry:** Customers have no independent way to verify quoted prices.
- **Inventory Opacity:** There is no real-time awareness of what is in stock.
- **No Search Capability:** Customers who do not visit physically have no way to know what parts are available.
- **Data Vulnerability:** Physical ledgers are exposed to risks of loss, damage, and unauthorized access.
- **Limited Business Intelligence:** No way to generate reports on sales trends or revenue patterns.

### 4.7.3 Security Testing

**SQL Injection Prevention:**

SQL injection is one of the most dangerous web application vulnerabilities. It occurs when attackers inject malicious SQL code through user inputs. The definitive prevention is **PDO prepared statements**.

**Vulnerable (DO NOT USE):**

// NEVER do this  
\$sql = "SELECT \* FROM users WHERE email = '" . \$\_POST\['email'\] . "'";

**Secure (SPAREPLUG IMPLEMENTATION):**

\$stmt = \$pdo->prepare("SELECT \* FROM users WHERE email = :email");  
\$stmt->execute(\['email' => \$email\]);  
\$user = \$stmt->fetch(PDO::FETCH_ASSOC);

All user input fields were tested with malicious payloads including ' OR '1'='1, '; DROP TABLE users; --, and &lt;script&gt;alert('XSS')&lt;/script&gt;. In every case, prepared statements treated input as data, not executable code.

**XSS Prevention:**

All user-generated content displayed in HTML is escaped using htmlspecialchars():

echo htmlspecialchars(\$product\['description'\], ENT_QUOTES, 'UTF-8');

# CHAPTER FIVE: DISCUSSION OF RESULTS, CONCLUSION, AND RECOMMENDATIONS

## 5.1 Introduction

This final chapter brings the research to a close. It presents a comprehensive discussion of the results achieved against the stated objectives, evaluates the extent to which the hypotheses were supported, reflects on the challenges encountered, acknowledges the limitations, and offers recommendations.

## 5.2 Discussion of Results

### 5.2.1 Achievement of Objectives

**Objective 1:** Examine existing manual processes and identify causes of inefficiency. - **Fully achieved.** Five core inefficiencies were documented through direct observation, interviews, and document review.

**Objective 2:** Design a user-friendly web platform with real-time search and price comparison. - **Fully achieved.** The SparePlug platform was designed and implemented with full search, filtering, and side-by-side price comparison capabilities.

**Objective 3:** Implement a backend system that automates stock tracking, price updates, and vendor management. - **Fully achieved.** The PHP/MySQL backend provides real-time inventory updates and a complete vendor management dashboard.

**Objective 4:** Assess the impact of the digital platform on customer satisfaction and competitive advantage. - **Substantially achieved.** Usability testing produced an 87.6% satisfaction score. Formal longitudinal measurement is recommended for the production phase.

**Objective 5:** Evaluate usability and adoption rate. - **Substantially achieved.** All participants completed core tasks successfully. Full adoption rate evaluation requires a longer production deployment.

### 5.2.2 Evaluation of Hypotheses

**H₀₁ vs H₁₁:** The null hypothesis that manual processes are not associated with inefficiency is **rejected**. Direct observation confirmed that manual processes directly cause pricing inconsistencies, inventory opacity, slow service, and data vulnerability. H₁₁ is accepted.

**H₀₂ vs H₁₂:** The null hypothesis that the web-based system has no effect on customer satisfaction is **rejected**. Usability testing results (87.6% average score) and participant feedback provide strong directional evidence that the platform improves satisfaction and efficiency. H₁₂ is accepted.

### 5.2.3 Key Technical Achievements

**Architecture:** Three-tier client-server architecture with clean separation between presentation (Tailwind CSS), application (PHP), and data (MySQL) layers.

**Security:** PDO prepared statements for every query; htmlspecialchars() for XSS prevention; bcrypt password hashing; role-based access control. 100% security test pass rate.

**Database:** 15 tables following 3NF with proper indexes, foreign keys, and ACID compliance.

**Mobile-First Design:** Fully responsive interface accessible on smartphones - the primary device for the target population.

### 5.2.4 Design Decisions and Rationale

**Connector Model (No In-App Payments):** SparePlug connects buyers and vendors but does not process payments within the platform. Transactions are completed through direct means (Mobile Money, cash on delivery). This reflects Cameroonian consumer preferences and allowed the project to focus resources on the core value proposition of parts availability and price comparison.

**WhatsApp as Primary Communication Channel:** Instead of building an in-app messaging system, SparePlug integrates WhatsApp, phone, and email. With 11.4 million active Mobile Money subscriptions in Cameroon (ART, 2024) and extremely high WhatsApp usage, this reflects natural communication habits and reduces adoption friction.

**Sequential Module Development (Admin → Vendor → Buyer):** This dependency-driven order ensured that each layer was solid before building upon it, reducing integration issues.

## 5.3 Challenges Encountered and Solutions

**Challenge 1: Limited Academic Literature on Cameroonian Auto Aftermarket.** Some staff at John Automobile were initially hesitant to share detailed inventory information due to concerns about local competition. The researcher explained the academic nature of the project, assured confidentiality, and demonstrated the system's security features. Cooperation was successfully secured.

**Challenge 2: Intermittent Internet Connectivity at Mvog-Ada.** Testing the system in Mvog-Ada was challenging due to unstable Wi-Fi. For the academic demonstration, the system was hosted on a free cloud platform. For production, the researcher recommends implementing **Progressive Web App (PWA)** technologies and **Service Workers** for offline-first caching.

**Challenge 3: Balancing Feature Scope with Academic Timeline.** A multi-vendor marketplace with full feature parity against global platforms would require months beyond the internship period. The researcher applied a **Minimum Viable Product (MVP)** approach - implementing all core features completely rather than many features partially.

**Challenge 4: Data Triangulation from Multiple Sources.** Managing data from interviews, observations, questionnaires, and secondary sources simultaneously required careful organization. The researcher used a structured approach: interviews for qualitative depth, questionnaires for breadth, observation for objective validation, and literature for theoretical grounding.

## 5.4 Limitations of the Study

- **Limited Usability Testing Sample:** Testing was conducted with only 5 participants. A larger sample would provide more statistically significant conclusions.
- **Short Deployment Period:** The system was tested during the final weeks of the internship. A longer deployment would allow for longitudinal data on usage patterns.
- **No In-App Payment Integration:** The connector model means the platform does not capture transaction data, limiting revenue analytics.
- **Internet Dependency:** The current version requires active internet connectivity, limiting accessibility in rural areas.

## 5.5 Conclusion

This research has successfully demonstrated that designing and implementing a web-based car parts availability and price comparison system for John Automobile in Yaoundé is technically feasible, practically valuable, and aligned with the needs of the local market.

The **SparePlug** platform delivers a functional, secure, and user-friendly digital marketplace that directly addresses the inefficiencies identified in the manual system. It is grounded in established theoretical frameworks - the **Technology Acceptance Model (TAM)** and the **DeLone and McLean Information Systems Success Model** - and validated through comprehensive testing.

The research contributes to the broader conversation about digital transformation in developing economies, demonstrating that even small and medium enterprises in informal urban markets like Mvog-Ada can benefit from web technology when the solution is carefully designed to match local needs, preferences, and constraints.

The journey from a paper ledger in a crowded Yaoundé warehouse to a web-accessible digital catalog is not just a technical achievement - it is a statement about possibility. SparePlug shows that with the right combination of research, design, and implementation, digital tools can serve the people who need them most, in the places where they need them most.
